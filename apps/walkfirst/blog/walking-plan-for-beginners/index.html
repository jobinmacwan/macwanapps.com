A 4-week walking plan for beginners: from 10 minutes to 150 a week

November 24, 2026 · Jobin Macwan · 4 min read

In short: start with a 10-minute walk on 5 days a week, then add 5 minutes a day each week. By week 4 you're walking 30 minutes on 5 days, the 150 minutes a week health guidelines recommend for adults. Walk at a pace where you can talk but not sing, and tie each walk to something you already do every day.

If you haven't exercised in a while, "150 minutes a week" can sound like a different person's life. You don't need to get there on day one. This plan starts small enough to say yes to on your worst day and builds up over four weeks.

The 4-week walking plan

Walk on 5 days a week. Which 5 doesn't matter, and rest days are part of the plan.

WeekEach walkWeekly totalFocus
Week 110 minutes, 5 days50 minutesJust show up. Comfortable pace.
Week 215 minutes, 5 days75 minutesSame time every day, so it becomes routine.
Week 320 minutes, 5 days100 minutesPick up the pace: talk, but don't sing.
Week 430 minutes, 5 days (one walk, or three 10-minute walks)150 minutesYou've reached the guideline.

Week 4 is the biggest jump, so it's fine to split each day's 30 minutes into three 10-minute walks. The CDC says activity can be broken up into smaller chunks of time and still counts. (CDC: Adult Activity) If a week felt hard, repeat it before moving on. Finishing the plan in six weeks is still finishing it.

Before you start

Moderate activity like brisk walking is safe for most people. The CDC advises talking with your doctor first if you have a chronic condition such as heart disease, arthritis or diabetes, and before starting vigorous activity if you've been inactive. (CDC: Adding Physical Activity as an Adult) Comfortable shoes are the only equipment you need.

How fast should a beginner walk?

Weeks 1 and 2 are about the habit, so any pace is fine. From week 3, aim for brisk: about 2.5 miles per hour (4 km/h) or faster, the speed the CDC counts as moderate intensity. The easy check is the talk test: you can hold a conversation, but you couldn't sing. (CDC: Measuring intensity) For why that pace matters, see does walking count as exercise?

How to make the walks stick

  1. Anchor it to something you already do. After lunch, after dinner, or straight after work before you sit down. An anchor beats a reminder. After-dinner walks also lower blood sugar after the meal; see walking after eating.
  2. Make starting easy. Shoes by the door, a default route, no decisions. The walk you don't have to plan is the one that happens.
  3. Track it. Ticking off a day, or watching a step count climb, is surprisingly motivating. Five ticks a week is the goal, not perfection.
  4. Have a minimum. On bad days, walk for 5 minutes. You'll often keep going, and if you don't, the habit survived.
  5. Put the walk before the phone. If your free time usually goes to scrolling, make the walk come first.

After week 4

Keep the 150 minutes, and add the other half of the guideline: muscle-strengthening activity on 2 days a week. Going beyond 150 minutes brings even more health benefit, so a longer weekend walk is a natural next step. (WHO: Physical activity) If you think in steps rather than minutes, read how many steps a day you really need to pick a goal that fits.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a beginner walk each day?
Start with 10 minutes on 5 days a week and add 5 minutes a day each week. After about four weeks you'll be at 30 minutes, 5 days a week, which is 150 minutes, the weekly amount health guidelines recommend.
How fast should I walk as a beginner?
Any pace is fine for the first two weeks. After that, aim for brisk walking, about 2.5 miles per hour (4 km/h) or faster, where you can talk but not sing.
Can I split my daily walk into shorter walks?
Yes. The CDC says activity can be broken into smaller chunks, so three 10-minute walks count the same as one 30-minute walk.
Should I see a doctor before starting a walking plan?
Brisk walking is safe for most people. The CDC advises talking with your doctor first if you have a chronic condition such as heart disease, arthritis or diabetes.
