Doomscrolling: why your brain can't stop — and the fix that isn't willpower

August 10, 2026 · Jobin Macwan · 5 min read

It's 11:40pm. You're tired. You've been looking at bad news and other people's opinions for forty minutes, you're not enjoying it, and you keep scrolling anyway. That gap — not enjoying it, continuing anyway — is the signature of doomscrolling, and it's worth understanding why it happens, because the explanation tells you exactly how to fix it.

Two systems, working against you

First: variable rewards. Feeds are slot machines. Most posts are noise, but occasionally one genuinely delights or enrages you, and unpredictable rewards are precisely the schedule that produces the most persistent behavior — this has been standard behavioral science since the 1950s. Your thumb isn't weak; it's correctly trained.

Second: negativity bias. Human attention evolved to prioritize threats. Alarming information feels urgent and important even when it's neither actionable nor new. Combine a slot machine with a threat detector and you get a loop your rational brain isn't invited to.

Why willpower-based fixes fail

Every standard fix — "just be mindful," greyscale mode, screen-time reminders you dismiss in one tap — asks your tired evening brain to out-argue a system built by thousands of engineers to win that argument. iOS Screen Time limits fail for one specific reason: the "Ignore Limit" button is right there, and pressing it costs nothing. I wrote more about that in how much screen time is too much.

The fix is friction, not discipline

You don't beat an effortless loop with effort; you beat it by making the loop not effortless. Every additional step between impulse and feed dramatically cuts how often you follow through. In order of strength:

  • Remove the apps from your first home screen. Search-only access adds two seconds — enough to notice yourself.
  • Log out at night. A login screen at 11pm is a surprisingly effective bouncer.
  • Make access cost something real. This is the approach I ended up building into WalkFirst: my chosen apps stay locked until I've hit a step goal. The feed still exists — but the price of admission is a walk, and at 11:40pm that price is exactly high enough.

What to expect

The first two or three days feel itchy — you'll catch your thumb reaching for an app that isn't where it was. That itch fades fast, and what replaces it is the mildly disorienting discovery of how much evening you actually have. You don't need to quit your phone. You need the effortless thing to take effort, and the healthy thing to be the path of least resistance.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stop doomscrolling at night?
Add friction before the feed: move social apps off your home screen, log out after each session, or use an app blocker that makes access conditional. At night specifically, charge your phone outside the bedroom — the distance is the intervention.
Why do I doomscroll even when I'm not enjoying it?
Feeds combine variable rewards (occasionally great posts on an unpredictable schedule) with your brain's negativity bias (threat-flavored content feels urgent). That combination sustains scrolling independent of enjoyment — which is why willpower alone rarely fixes it.
Is doomscrolling bad for your mental health?
Heavy late-night scrolling of negative content is consistently associated with worse mood and delayed sleep. The clearest practical harm for most people is displaced sleep — which then lowers next-day self-control and makes more scrolling likelier.
