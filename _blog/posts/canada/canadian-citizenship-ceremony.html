In short: the citizenship ceremony is the last step for most applicants, and it usually lasts a few hours, whether virtual or in person. Bring your invitation, your PR card (even if expired) or Confirmation of Permanent Residence, and two pieces of ID, at least one with your photo and signature. Wear business attire; traditional and religious clothing is welcome. You take the oath, sign the Oath or Affirmation of Citizenship form, and leave as a Canadian citizen.

After the test, and sometimes an interview, comes the part everyone looks forward to. The invitation can arrive with only a week's notice, though, so it helps to know what's coming before it lands. Everything below comes from Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) on canada.ca.

When does the ceremony invitation arrive?

IRCC invites you to the ceremony after it approves your application, and sends the invitation at least 1 week before the ceremony date. How long you wait depends on current processing times and your situation, for example whether you had to take the test or your application is non-routine. If the "Citizenship ceremony" section of your status tracker says In progress, IRCC is scheduling your ceremony. (IRCC: When to go to the ceremony)

The invitation usually comes by email from an address ending in "@cic.gc.ca", so add that to your safe senders and check your spam folder. Without an email address, IRCC may call you (the call can show as "Unknown caller" or a number outside Canada) or send the invitation by mail.

Your invitation tells you the date and time, the format (virtual or in person), the video link or the location, and the ceremony's language: mostly English, mostly French, or bilingual.

Virtual or in person?

IRCC may invite you to either a virtual ceremony by video conference or an in-person ceremony. If you'd prefer the other format, you can ask to reschedule, but you can request a format change only once, and you'll get a new date rather than keep the same one. (IRCC: Ceremony formats)

How long is the citizenship ceremony?

IRCC says both virtual and in-person ceremonies last a few hours, and your invitation gives the arrival time and length. Much of that is registration: everyone has to be checked in before the ceremony starts, and there can be over 100 candidates. At a large virtual ceremony you may wait up to 30 minutes before you're let in. Plan for the whole morning or afternoon. (IRCC: What to expect at the ceremony)

What to bring to the citizenship ceremony

Your ceremony invitationEveryone
Proof of permanent residenceYour PR card, even if it's expired, or your Confirmation of Permanent Residence (IMM 5292 or IMM 5688)
2 pieces of ID (adults)At least one with both your photo and signature, for example a passport, driver's licence or health card. If one of them is your PR card, the other must be issued by the federal, provincial or territorial government.
Signed consent formThe permission release and consent form from your invitation: always for in person, and for virtual if your invitation includes one
Virtual onlyYour virtual seat number (on the first page of the invitation) and a pair of scissors to cut up your PR card at the start

Minors and the parents who applied for them need their own ID; IRCC's page lists what's accepted for each. Foreign ID must be government-issued, with a translation and translator's affidavit if it isn't in English or French. (IRCC: What to bring to the ceremony)

Keep your Confirmation of Permanent Residence or Record of Landing. IRCC notes you may need it later, for example when you apply for Old Age Security.

What to wear

IRCC asks for business attire that suits the occasion. You're also welcome to wear traditional, religious or ceremonial clothing, head coverings, the colours red and white, a medical mask or a face covering. At in-person ceremonies, skip perfume and cologne: scented products aren't allowed because they can make others unwell.

What happens at the ceremony

  1. Registration. An official checks your identity (even if you wear a face covering) and asks questions to confirm nothing prevents you from becoming a citizen. In person, they collect your PR card; at a virtual ceremony, you cut it up with scissors.
  2. The ceremony. Speeches, sometimes special guests, then you take the oath of citizenship and sing O Canada. IRCC encourages you to practise both beforehand, and to repeat the oath in both official languages if you can.
  3. The form. You sign the Oath or Affirmation of Citizenship form, which confirms you took the oath. At a virtual ceremony you must sign it on the day, not the day before or after, and email it back as the invitation explains.
  4. Your certificate. If you chose a paper certificate, you receive it at an in-person ceremony. If you chose an e-certificate, IRCC emails you instructions to download it after the ceremony, once it has your signed form.

You can swear or affirm the oath. Swearing refers to a religious belief; affirming doesn't. The oath today names His Majesty King Charles the Third, King of Canada.

Virtual ceremony etiquette

  • Pick a quiet, safe room, keep your head and shoulders visible, and stay seated throughout, even for the oath and O Canada.
  • No virtual backgrounds. A plain wall, a Canadian flag or red and white are fine.
  • Don't share the link with anyone, even family who applied with you, and don't take photos or recordings during registration or the ceremony. IRCC does allow a personal photo of you and your family with the presiding official and special guests.
  • Use a laptop, desktop, tablet or phone with a working camera and audio, and test the video platform beforehand.

At an in-person ceremony, candidates stand for the oath and O Canada if they can, and you may take photos of yourself and your family for personal use.

Who takes the oath, and can family come?

Everyone 14 and older takes the oath. Children under 14 don't have to, but they're welcome to attend with the parent or guardian who applied for them. Family and friends can come to celebrate, and members of the public can attend most in-person ceremonies. (IRCC: Who has to take the oath)

Can you reschedule the ceremony?

Yes, for reasons such as travel, illness, child care, wanting a different format or language, or being outside Canada. Reply to the office that sent your invitation (or use IRCC's web form) within 30 days after the ceremony date in your invitation, and explain why. If it's your first request, IRCC reschedules you and sends a final invitation, which it calls your last chance to attend, so choose the new date carefully.
