In short: yes. Brisk walking, about 2.5 miles per hour (4 km/h) or faster, counts as moderate-intensity exercise, the kind health guidelines count. Adults need at least 150 minutes a week, such as 30 minutes on 5 days, plus muscle-strengthening activity on 2 days. Short walks add up, and any amount is better than none.

Walking can feel too easy to count. It isn't running, there's no gym, and you've been doing it since you were one. But the major health guidelines are clear: walking at the right pace is real exercise, and for most people it's the simplest way to meet the weekly target.

When walking counts as exercise

Guidelines count moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity. The CDC lists "walking briskly (2.5 miles per hour or faster)" as its first example of moderate-intensity activity, next to recreational swimming and doubles tennis. (CDC: Measuring physical activity intensity)

You don't need a speedometer. Use the talk test: at moderate intensity you can talk, but not sing. If you can't say more than a few words without pausing for breath, you've reached vigorous intensity. On a 0 to 10 effort scale, where 0 is sitting and 10 is all-out, moderate is about 5 or 6.

A slow stroll still beats sitting, but if you want your walks to count toward the guideline, walk like you're slightly late for something.

How much walking do you need a week?

Both the CDC and the World Health Organization use the same baseline for adults: at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix. The CDC adds 2 days of muscle-strengthening activity a week. Going beyond 150 minutes brings even more benefit.

That's 30 minutes of brisk walking on 5 days. It sounds manageable, yet the WHO estimates that 31% of adults worldwide, about 1.8 billion people, don't reach it.

Do short walks add up?

Yes. The CDC says you don't have to do your 150 minutes all at once: "You can spread your activity out during the week and break it up into smaller chunks of time." Three 10-minute walks count the same as one 30-minute walk, and short walks after meals have their own benefit for blood sugar (see walking after eating).

Both the CDC and the WHO also stress that some activity is better than none. If 150 minutes feels far away, a 10-minute walk today is still a step toward it.

A week of walking that meets the guideline

This is the CDC's own first example of a week that meets both targets:

Total: 150 minutes of moderate activity and 2 days of muscle strengthening. (CDC: Adding Physical Activity as an Adult) If you're starting from very little, build up to it gradually with a 4-week walking plan for beginners.

What walking doesn't cover

Walking takes care of the aerobic part of the guideline, but not the muscle-strengthening part. Two short sessions a week that work the major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms) complete the picture. Bodyweight squats, push-ups against a wall and carrying heavy shopping all count as a start.

What about steps?

The guidelines are written in minutes, not steps, so there's no official daily step number. If you'd rather think in steps, read how many steps a day you really need and where the 10,000-step goal came from.
